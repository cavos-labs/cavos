An [embedded wallet](/embedded-wallet) lives inside your application. The user signs in the way they already do. The product resolves an on-chain account they can pay, receive, and sign with — no browser extension, no seed phrase, no trip to another app.

**Non-custodial** and **self-custodial** are claims about that account, not about the login modal. This guide is the developer test: who can sign, which key models survive that test, and how to verify a vendor’s wording. The [custody](/custody) page is the legal side — MiCA, FinCEN, California, and the other regimes. This page does not repeat those statutes. The [embedded wallet API](/blog/embedded-wallet-api) guide is the surface map. The [React integration](/blog/how-to-add-an-embedded-wallet) is the install.

Cavos, LLC is a software and SaaS company. The wallet is the main product: a device-native, self-custodial embedded wallet. Signing keys are created and used on the user’s device. Cavos cannot see them, sign with them, or move funds.

## What is a non-custodial embedded wallet?

A **non-custodial embedded wallet** is an in-app crypto wallet the provider cannot spend from. The wallet still lives inside the application: the user signs in, the product resolves an on-chain account, and approval happens in the app UI. Self-custody is a separate fact. The signing key is created and used where the vendor cannot produce a valid signature alone. Calling a product non-custodial does not make it so — the test is who can move the assets.

Non-custodial embedded wallets (plural) are the same design at product scale: every user account follows that test. Embedded describes where the wallet appears. It does not decide custody. The [embedded wallet](/embedded-wallet) page is the category definition; this page is the custody test. The legal control test is on [custody](/custody).

## Who can sign is who controls the key

A wallet is an on-chain account plus the means to authorize transactions from it. Custody is the answer to one question: **who can produce a valid signature without the user present?**

If the provider can complete a transaction alone, or holds the means of access, the wallet is custodial in practice — even when the docs say “non-custodial” or “end-user owned.” If the provider cannot see the key, cannot sign with it, and cannot move funds, the wallet is self-custodial. Calling a product non-custodial does not make it so. The test is who can move the assets.

Embedded is a UX label. Login selects the user, the SDK looks up or creates an on-chain address, and approval happens in the app UI. The same modal is used for hosted signing, MPC shares, and device-native keys. The [providers comparison](/blog/embedded-wallet-providers) is the named-vendor shortlist. Worked one-to-one pages live under [compare](/compare).

Authentication should select which user this is. It should not, by itself, authorize a spend. When email magic-link login is enough to move funds, the provider usually holds the signing capability. Collapse login and signing and you have chosen a hosted model.

A self-custodial embedded wallet is one the provider cannot spend from. The signing key is created and used on the user’s device and is not reconstructed from provider-held shares. That is the definition this site already uses. Everything below is how to check it.

## Key models — and which are non-custodial in practice

When a vendor says “embedded wallet SDK,” ask where the signing key lives. Five designs cover almost every product in the category.

### Device-native keys

The wallet’s account key is derived on the user’s device from a MasterDEK. At rest that DEK is protected by the platform’s non-extractable key: WebCrypto in the browser, the OS keystore on React Native. The device signs. A new device restores the same MasterDEK instead of being added on-chain.

This is non-custodial **when the provider cannot see the DEK, cannot sign with it, and cannot move funds**. Isolation comes from the platform primitive, not from a slogan. Those primitives have limits: WebCrypto cannot hold a stark key, so outside the Cavos vault the Starknet key is re-derived into memory for the session, and the SDK does not enforce non-extractability on Node or other server runtimes. Ask where the key is actually used, not only where it is created.

### Passkeys

Passkeys (WebAuthn) are often described as “the wallet.” They are not one thing. A passkey can sign spends, approve a device, or unwrap a key the vendor also holds. Those are three different products.

On Starknet, Solana, and Stellar in Cavos, a WebAuthn PRF credential encrypts a copy of the wallet’s MasterDEK, which Cavos stores and cannot open. That is not 2FA: anyone with the synced passkey (iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager) can spend, which is also how a synced passkey recovers the wallet on a new device. When a vendor says “passkey wallet,” ask which of those jobs the passkey does. A passkey that unwraps a key the vendor can also use is not self-custody.

### MPC / TSS shares

The private key is split into shares. Signing reconstructs or jointly computes a signature — typically some mix of the user’s device, the provider’s network, and a recovery factor. Threshold signature schemes (TSS) are the usual production shape: no single share is the key, and a quorum produces a signature.

MPC is a legitimate design. It is not the same as “the provider never has a key.” The provider holds a share used in reconstruction. You are trusting the share holders, the protocol, and whoever can coerce enough shares. Export is often supported — a feature and a different threat model. It is non-custodial only if the vendor cannot reach the threshold without the user. Cavos does not split or reconstruct a signing key with MPC.

### TEE / enclave-held keys

A secure enclave (AWS Nitro, or a vendor HSM) holds key material that is supposed to be usable only inside a measured environment. Remote enclaves are not the user’s device. Server-side signing is the point of that design.

Custody depends on who holds the credentials that authorize the enclave. If the vendor’s operators can approve a sign request, the enclave is a hosted signer with better hardware. If the enclave cannot be asked to sign a spend in the ordinary flow, you are closer to a wrap than to a remote hot wallet.

Cavos uses an enclave only for opt-in hardware-isolated social recovery: an AWS Nitro Enclave with pinned attestation, off by default, which can only unwrap a wallet’s MasterDEK on Starknet, Solana, and Stellar. It is not general server-side signing. The docs call that path non-custodial, and not trustless.

### Hosted or delegated keys

The provider, or an HSM it operates, can produce a signature. Email magic-link login often sits on this model: access to the inbox is enough to spend. If the vendor can sign without the user, the wallet is custodial. Some vendors ship a non-custodial embedded path and, separately, an optional custodial wallet. Read both.

In practice: device-native keys are non-custodial when the vendor cannot see or use the key (the Cavos default). Passkeys are non-custodial when they unwrap a key the vendor cannot open. MPC / TSS is non-custodial only if the vendor cannot reach the threshold without the user. TEE and enclave-held keys follow the authorizing credential — remote signing is hosted unless the enclave cannot be asked to spend. Hosted or delegated keys are custodial. “Embedded wallets” as a search phrase does not pick a row.

## A checklist to verify a vendor’s claim

Write the answers down before you compare SDKs. Marketing copy will not.

**Can the vendor sign alone?** Who can produce a valid signature without the user present? Ask for the ordinary spend path, not a slide about “user-controlled keys.” If a support tool, an admin API, or a recovery share is enough to move funds, that is the answer.

**Key export.** Can the user export a private key or seed, or only restore the same address on a new device? Addresses are derived differently across vendors. Migration is a project. Export is a feature and a different threat model: a seed you can copy is a seed an attacker can copy. Cavos restores the MasterDEK on a new device. It does not advertise a seed or private-key export.

**What happens if the vendor disappears?** If signing requires a provider share, a remote enclave credential, or a hosted HSM, disappearance is a lockout unless export or a documented break-glass exists. If the key lives on the device, or can be restored from a factor the user holds, the address can still spend — assuming the user can still reach the chain. Relayers and paymasters transport, sponsor, or submit. They are not signers.

**Recovery path.** Recovery is how a new device restores the means to sign. Ask what it unwraps, who can trigger it, and whether it can be arranged after every device is gone. A provider that cannot sign in the ordinary flow but can restore a spend key in an incident is a different fact pattern than one that never can. Counsel has to read the recovery path. That is a product fact as well as a [legal](/custody) one.

**Audit status.** Ask what was audited. On Starknet, Cavos wallets are the standard audited OpenZeppelin account contract (AccountUpgradeable). The audit covers OpenZeppelin’s contract, not a custom Cavos account. Cavos does not ship its own account implementation. An enclave attestation, an SDK review, and an on-chain account audit are three different artifacts. Do not treat “audited” as a custody proof.

## Recovery trade-offs

A device that is lost, wiped, or replaced takes its key with it. Recovery is a second authority set up *before* that happens. None of those paths can be arranged after every device is gone.

The usual designs:

- **Another signed-in device.** Works while a phone still holds the key; useless after the only device is gone.
- **A synced passkey.** Fast restore on a new device. Anyone with that passkey can spend. Treat iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager as the recovery root, not as a second factor.
- **A hardware-isolated enclave.** Opt-in wrap of the same DEK. Better than a reconstructable server share if the enclave can only unwrap, under pinned attestation. Still not trustless.
- **On-chain social recovery or extra device signers.** A new device is added as an on-chain signer, or guardians rotate the account. Guardians, delays, and on-chain policy become part of custody.

Cavos does not use the last design. There is no on-chain device-signer list and no on-chain social recovery. On Starknet, Solana, and Stellar, a new device restores the MasterDEK and derives the same spend key. It does not add an on-chain signer.

Off-chain restore keeps the on-chain account simple — one key, the chain’s native model — and moves recovery to passkeys and an opt-in enclave. You give up on-chain guardian rotation. You keep the rule that Cavos cannot sign. If every device and every restore factor is gone, Cavos cannot recover the wallet.

## How Cavos does it

The public developer surface is `@cavos/kit` (`@cavos/kit/react`, `@cavos/kit/react-native`). Login produces a stable identity for a registry lookup; it never signs a transaction. There is no MPC and no hosted spend key. The account key is derived on the device from a MasterDEK that WebCrypto or the OS keystore protects at rest. Cavos cannot see it.

Each adapter keeps the chain’s native account model:

- **[Starknet](/embedded-starknet-wallet).** An audited OpenZeppelin account contract — AccountUpgradeable v3.0.0 — controlled by one stark key derived from the MasterDEK and `appSalt`. Cavos does not ship its own account implementation. There are no on-chain device signers. Execution is paymaster-sponsored. Connect never deploys; the first execute deploys and runs atomically.
- **[Solana](/embedded-solana-wallet).** A native Ed25519 system account. The spend key is HKDF of the same MasterDEK. A relayer co-signs as fee payer. The address is an ordinary Solana public key, not a PDA.
- **[Stellar](/embedded-stellar-wallet).** A classic G… account, not a Soroban contract account. The spend key is one Ed25519 key, HKDF of the MasterDEK (`cavos-ed25519-stellar-v1`, salted by `appSalt`). A new device restores that DEK and derives the same key. The relayer can sponsor reserves and fee-bumps.

A new device restores the MasterDEK off-chain with a passkey or an opt-in hardware-isolated enclave. It does not add a signer on-chain. `needs-device-approval` means this device has no key yet. Restore the MasterDEK; the status is not an on-chain device list.

The kit documents three paths: another signed-in device, a passkey, and opt-in hardware-isolated recovery in an AWS Nitro Enclave that can only unwrap a MasterDEK. If every device and every restore factor is gone, Cavos cannot recover the wallet.

That is the product. Integrating a self-custodial SDK does not settle exchange, on-ramp, or payment licensing. Recovery paths count. The control test under those regimes is on the [custody](/custody) page.
